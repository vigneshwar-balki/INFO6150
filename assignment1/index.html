The Royal Legacy

The Mysore Dasara, known as Naada Habba — the State Festival — stands as one of India's most magnificent celebrations of good over evil, dating its royal patronage to 1610 CE under the Wadiyar dynasty. When the great Wadiyar kings reclaimed Mysore from the Nayakas, they enshrined Dasara as the premier expression of royal authority and divine grace, transforming a religious observance into a ten-day spectacle of unparalleled grandeur. Every year as the monsoon retreats and the Chamundi Hills gleam in autumn light, Mysore transforms itself into a city of gold and light.

Raja Wadiyar I first instituted the grand procession from the Mysore Palace in the early seventeenth century, fusing the classical Vijayadashami tradition with the elaborate court ceremonies of the Deccan. The festival grew under successive rulers — most notably Krishnaraja Wadiyar III and Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV — who added the iconic elephant procession, the illumination of the entire palace with nearly 100,000 bulbs, and the grand Jamboo Savari tableau that now draws millions of pilgrims and tourists alike. The golden howdah atop the lead elephant carries the idol of Goddess Chamundeshwari, patron deity of Mysore, through streets lined with jasmine and marigold.

Even through periods of political transition — the eventual merger of the Mysore Kingdom with the Indian Union in 1947, and the abolition of privy purses in 1971 — the festival never dimmed. The Government of Karnataka assumed patronage, and the Mysore District Administration has faithfully maintained the regal traditions, ensuring that schoolchildren, folk artists, armed forces contingents, and decorated elephants continue to march down the Mysore Palace road each Vijayadashami. This archive exists to preserve every layer of that unbroken living heritage.

Year Chronicles

Dasara 2023 — The Jamboo Savari Returns in Full Glory

24 October 2023

After years shadowed by pandemic restrictions and scaled-down ceremonies, the 2023 Jamboo Savari reclaimed its legendary scale. The magnificent elephant Abhimanyu bore the 750-kilogram golden howdah carrying the idol of Goddess Chamundeshwari from the Mysore Palace to Bannimantap grounds, flanked by nine caparisoned elephants and a procession of over 10,000 participants. Tableaux from all 30 Karnataka districts rolled past cheering crowds estimated at nearly 8 lakh — the highest since 2018.

Chief Minister Siddaramaiah presided over the torch-lighting ceremony on the palace steps at dusk, as cannon volleys thundered from Chamundi Hill. The Mysore City Police, mounted cavalry, and NCC cadets provided a martial backdrop to the civilian revelry. Cultural troupes from Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Tamil Nadu shared the parade ground, underlining Dasara's identity as a festival of all of India. Fireworks over the palace illuminated the night sky until well past midnight.

Dasara 2022 — Yuva Dasara and the Cultural Resurgence

5 October 2022

The 2022 edition was defined by Yuva Dasara, a youth-focused cultural programme initiated by the state government to ensure the next generation engages meaningfully with the festival's artistic heritage. Over ten evenings, the open-air stage at the Mysore Palace Grounds hosted classical Bharatanatyam, Carnatic music concerts, Yakshagana theatrical performances, and contemporary fusion acts performed by artists under the age of thirty-five. Nearly forty stage programmes attracted audiences of two lakh across the fortnight.

Particularly notable was the performance by young Mysore-based sculptor Kiran Shastri, whose sand-art installation depicting the entire 1610 Wadiyar court drew national media coverage. The Karnataka Chitrakala Parishath sponsored a live painting marathon in which sixty artists simultaneously rendered scenes from Dasara mythology across a 200-metre canvas erected along JLB Road. The archive holds digital scans of several panels donated to us by the artists.

Dasara 2019 — The Last Grand Pre-Pandemic Celebration

8 October 2019

None who attended the 2019 Dasara could have imagined it would be the last unrestricted grand celebration for years. A record 7.5 million visitors descended on Mysore across the ten-day festival — straining every guesthouse and homestay in the district yet generating over ₹800 crore in economic activity. The exhibition grounds at Doddakere Maidan hosted 240 stalls; the food fair alone drew families who queued for hours to sample specialities from all of Karnataka's districts.

The Vijayadashami procession of 2019 featured a newly designed float for the Department of Ayush that incorporated live demonstrations of traditional medicine, and a robotics tableau from the Department of Electronics that drew thunderous applause from the stands opposite the palace. Elephant Arjuna, who had led the Jamboo Savari for fifteen consecutive years, made his final official appearance that October, retiring to the Nagarhole reserve the following January. His farewell was spontaneously marked by the crowd with a standing ovation along the entire three-kilometre route.

Historical Timeline

Key milestones in the history of Mysore Dasara from 1610 to 2023
Year Event Significance
1610 First royal Dasara under Raja Wadiyar I Established the festival as the official state celebration of the Mysore Kingdom, fusing court ceremony with Vijayadashami ritual.
1805 Krishnaraja Wadiyar III codifies the Jamboo Savari procession Formalised the elephant procession route from palace to Bannimantap, setting the template still followed today.
1902 Palace illumination introduced by Nalwadi Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV Electric bulbs first used to outline the palace facade, a tradition that grew to 100,000 bulbs by the mid-20th century.
1947 First Dasara post-Independence of India The royal celebrations continued seamlessly; Jaya Chamarajendra Wadiyar inaugurated the festival as both king and Rajpramukh.
1971 Karnataka Government assumes full patronage Following the 26th Constitutional Amendment, the state declared Dasara the official Naada Habba and allocated formal budget support.
1998 UNESCO acknowledges Mysore Dasara as a living heritage event International recognition spurred infrastructure investment and brought the festival to global tourist itineraries.
2020–2021 Pandemic-era symbolic Dasara Jamboo Savari conducted with only a handful of officials inside the palace compound; broadcast live nationally — first time in the festival's 400-year history held without public attendance.
2023 Full-scale revival — record 8-lakh crowd Largest public attendance since 2018; Yuva Dasara, cultural programmes, and digital archiving initiatives launched alongside the traditional procession.

Did you know?

The Mysore Palace is illuminated with exactly 97,000 bulbs every Sunday evening year-round — not just during Dasara. This tradition, begun by Maharaja Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV in 1902 when electricity first reached Mysore, makes it one of the earliest palaces in Asia to be electrically lit. During Dasara, additional floodlights raise the total to over 100,000 lights, visible from Chamundi Hill more than 13 km away.
